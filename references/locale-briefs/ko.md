## Audience and register

- A friendly coach in everyday Korean, formal but warm (`하십시오체`, decided below): short sentences, one idea per line, concrete verbs, no jargon and no English sentence shapes carried over word for word. A reader who knows no English must understand each line at first read, which rules out Konglish loanwords the source's own English shape might tempt a translator toward (`트랙하다` for "track", `스트릭` for "streak") where an ordinary Korean word already exists.
- **Drop the subject and the possessive wherever the referent is obvious.** Korean routinely omits "you"/"your" where English and Romanian require it; inserting `당신은`/`당신의`/`너의` on every line is the single most reliable "this was translated" tell in Korean UI copy, and Apple's own harvest never does it (Address and gender). 177 `en` keys carry a second-person possessive; the default move is to state the noun bare ("your streak" -> "연속 기록", not "당신의 연속 기록"), keeping an explicit possessive only for genuine contrast or ambiguity.
- Korean writes headline-register noun phrases without particles for titles and headers, and full formal sentences for body text and descriptions — see Header and label forms. A spaced, particle-heavy translation of a short English header reads as machine output.
- Imported shapes the editor flags. The first two columns are the marker list the coordinator appends to the editor prompt, target text only; the key column stays coordinator-side (`references/agent-protocol.md` `## Packet separation`). **The Korean examples are agent-authored illustrations, not shipped copy** — no `ko` string exists yet.

## Address and gender

- **Formal polite register (`하십시오체`, `-습니다`/`-ㅂ니다`), not `해요체`.** Every register-bearing string in the Apple `ko` harvest ends in `-합니다`/`-습니다`, and no `-해요` form appears anywhere: "‘%@’에서 알림을 보내고자 합니다." (`UserNotificationsServer.framework | Localizable.strings | USER_NOTIFICATION_PERMISSION_ALERT_TITLE`), "‘%@’에 스크린 타임 접근을 허용하면… 앱 및 웹사이트의 사용을 제한할 수도 있습니다." (`FamilyControlsAuthenticationUI.app | Localizable.strings | NOTIFICATION_AUTH_REQUEST_MESSAGE_INDIVIDUAL`), "나중에 건강 앱에서 건강 데이터 카테고리를 켤 수 있습니다." (`HealthUI.framework | HealthUI-Localizable.strings | AUTHORIZATION_DONT_ALLOW_ALERT_MESSAGE`). A fetched Apple Korean support guide confirms the same register outside the OS itself: "이 제어를 수정하면 이 페이지는 자동으로 업데이트됩니다." and section headings built on the `-기` nominalizer, "위젯 추가, 편집 및 제거하기" (https://support.apple.com/ko-kr/guide/iphone/set-up-screen-time-for-yourself-iphbfa595995/ios, fetched 2026-09-16). Register summary from the runtime harvest: `{"address": "formal polite 합니다; user = 사용자", "buttons": "noun forms", "case": "no letter case"}` (platform-terms facts). **Decided for this app's disclaimers, permission mocks, control forms and body sentences**, because the evidence for that genre is one-sided: nothing in the OS-chrome harvest supports `해요체` there. That harvest skews toward permission alerts and system chrome, the one Apple genre guaranteed to be maximally formal in any language; Apple's own coaching-copy analogue (Fitness+ achievement text) instead uses `-해요체` ("계속 움직여주세요!", Idioms and figurative language). This app's copy is overwhelmingly the permission/disclaimer/control-form genre the harvest actually covers, so `하십시오체` stays the working register throughout, including in the Idioms proposals — but whether short motivational/celebratory lines specifically should follow the coaching-copy register instead is tracked, not decided, in Open decisions `coaching_register`.
- **The reader is addressed as `사용자` ("the user") only where an explicit noun is unavoidable, never as `당신` or `너`.** Apple's own individual-access alert names the reader this way: "…스크린 타임 접근을 허용하면, 이 앱이 **사용자**의 활동 데이터를 보고…" — not "당신의 활동 데이터". No pronoun of any kind appears in the harvest. `당신` reads as a distancing, translated-pamphlet word in contemporary Korean UI; `너`/`네` is too familiar for an address register this formal. Default to dropping the subject entirely (see Audience and register); reach for `사용자` only when the sentence must name the reader explicitly to avoid ambiguity with a third party (e.g. a child's device in a Family Sharing context, which this app does not have).
- The app speaks about itself in the first person plural in 19 `en` units ("We couldn't load the data.", `component.errorView.message`; "We covered %@ with a streak freeze.", `streakFreeze.usedBody`). Korean formal register conventionally drops this subject too rather than inserting `저희`/`우리`: "데이터를 불러올 수 없습니다." reads more natural than "저희는 데이터를 불러올 수 없습니다." Keep an explicit `저희` only where the sentence needs to contrast the app with the user or a third party.
- **Gender.** Korean has no grammatical gender at all: nouns, adjectives and verbs carry no gender marking for the reader or for a third party, so none of the Romance-locale agreement machinery (predicate adjectives, participle endings, role-noun suffixes) applies here. The 44 reader-predicate `en` units (`you_are` 5, `are_you` 5, `you_showed_up` 1, `welcome_back` 1, `ready` 7, `focused` 19, `done` 15) need no gender strategy of any kind.
- The badge family's role nouns (`achievements.badge.hired.name` "Hired", `.salaried.name` "Salaried", `.rehired.name` "Rehired", `.clockedIn.name` "Clocked in", `.backOnShift.name` "Back on shift", `.permanent.name` "Permanent", `.tenure.name` "Tenure", plus `achievements.category.payroll.name` "Payroll" and `.shift.name` "The Shift") map onto ordinary Sino-Korean employment nouns (입사, 재입사, 월급제 전환, 첫 출근, 복귀, 근속, 급여, 근무) that are themselves unmarked for gender — Korean has no feminine/masculine agent-noun suffix to avoid here at all, unlike Dutch's `-ster` or Romanian's paired forms. This removes the one hotspot other locale briefs need a strategy for; the only remaining call is whether the workplace conceit travels at all (Open decisions `badge_conceit`).
- `user.gender.*` is a data field, not voice: `user.gender.female` "Female", `.male` "Male", `.other` "Other", `.preferNotToSay` "Prefer not to say" — seed 2026-09-16: "여성", "남성", "기타", "밝히지 않음". The stored profile gender (`Manifesting/Features/User/Domain/Entities/Gender.swift`) is not a copy input without a product decision.
- Grep aid, not a validator: `당신|너\b|네가|자기야`. A hit anywhere in app-owned copy is a register mismatch to flag; the string is almost never absent by coincidence.

## Control forms

Counts are `en` catalog keys whose `en` value is exactly the control word, the same match `scripts/term_audit.py` makes (en-inventory `## Control forms`); there is no `ko` copy to count. Preferred and role forms mirror `glossary/ko.json` `controls` exactly, role names as the glossary spells them, because the script reads the glossary, not this table.

**Local convention: a bare Sino-Korean noun, or a verbal noun built with the `-기` nominalizer — never a full imperative sentence, and Korean has no infinitive marker to reach for instead.** Apple's Korean iOS writes almost every button and menu item this way: 취소(Cancel), 확인(OK), 완료(Done), 계속(Continue), 저장(Save), 편집(Edit), 삭제(Delete), 허용/허용 안 함(Allow/Don't Allow), 닫기(Close, `닫다`+`-기`), 설정 열기(Open Settings, `열다`+`-기`), 설정으로 이동(Go to Settings, "movement to Settings"), 위젯 추가(Add Widget, "widget addition"), 위젯 검색(Search Widgets), 앱 공유(Share App), Face ID 요구(Require Face ID), 사용자화(Customize) — none of these is a conjugated command like "눌러 주세요" ("please press"). The fetched Apple support guide confirms the same pattern outside buttons, using `-기` for page headings ("위젯 추가, 편집 및 제거하기", "홈 화면에서 앱 및 위젯 사용자화하기"). This is the Korean parallel to Dutch's "bare imperative stem" rule and Romanian's infinitive-vs-imperative choice: Korean UI simply does not put a full sentence, formal or otherwise, on a button.

## Header and label forms

- **Nav titles, section headers, eyebrows and tab titles are bare noun phrases with particles dropped; body text, descriptions and toggles are full `-습니다` sentences.** Apple's own Korean headers confirm the pattern: "언어 및 지역" (Language & Region, no particle between the two nouns beyond the conjunction), "홈 화면 및 앱 보관함" (Home Screen & App Library). The 114 Title Case `en` keys and the 34 all-caps `en` keys (Case of 2-4 word strings; All-caps `en` strings) both collapse to this one Korean convention: a title-case English string such as "Set Your Hourly Wage" becomes the bare noun phrase "시급 설정" (`onboarding.hourlyWage.title`), and an all-caps caption such as "MOOD" (`insights.mood.mood`) becomes the plain noun "기분" — Korean has no letter case, so there is no separate "loud" orthographic form the way English or Dutch has, and any attempt to fake one (spacing letters out, adding emphasis marks) is a design cue for engineering (weight, size, colour), never a text transformation.
- Since Korean nouns do not inflect for number, the same header noun serves any count without a plural ending: Apple's own widget vocabulary reuses one word, "위젯", for both "Widget" and "Widgets" (`widget_noun.singular_label` and `.plural_header`, `SpringBoardHome.framework` and `SmartStackSettingsUI.framework`). This removes the Dutch-style singular/plural header choice entirely.
- **A short celebratory or headline caption may drop the sentence-final verb for a punchier nominal form while staying inside the decided formal register** (never `-해요`), the same way Apple's own headers drop particles: a badge `.earned` line such as "First session done." can read as the clipped "첫 세션 완료." rather than the fuller "첫 세션을 완료했습니다." Both are `하십시오체`-compatible; the choice is about rhythm on a one-line caption, not about politeness level, and body text longer than one line keeps the full sentence form.
- Toggles and state labels take the productive `-됨`/`-된` passive-nominal suffix on a Sino-Korean action noun, which does not inflect for the reader's gender or number at all: "잠김" (Locked), "선택됨" (Selected), "차단됨" (Blocked). This is a seed pattern (no single Apple `ko` citation covers every one of the 103 `en` participle/adjective state labels in this harvest), but it is the ordinary, productive way contemporary Korean UI marks a per-item completed or applied state, and it removes the gender-agreement problem the Romance locales need a strategy for (Address and gender). **This is a per-item state suffix, not a section-heading form**: a heading over a whole list of completed items (`completed_count_header`, Control forms) is a different job from a per-item badge, so it takes the bare noun "완료" instead of "완료됨" (Control forms).

## Numbers, dates, currency and punctuation

All values below are Foundation/ICU output for `ko_KR` (locale-formats, ICU 76.1.0.0 / CLDR 46.0.0.0), or the app's own `AppCurrency` path on that locale.

- **Plurals: one category, `other`.** ICU's `uplrules_getKeywords` and Xcode's `variationConfigFile.plist` agree: Korean has no `one`/`few`/`many` split at all, and every integer from 0 to 999,999 plus every fraction tested (0.5, 1.5, 2.5) selects `other` (locale-formats `plural`). This means: the 29 `en` plural keys each need exactly one Korean form, not two or three; the 6 keys that are a single `en` `stringUnit` but split into `one`/`few`/`other` in `ro` (`achievements.streak.body`, `.comebackBody`, `.headline`, `.milestoneBody`, `.nextMilestoneBody`, `.weekAccessibility`) need no extra branch in Korean either, since `other` is the only category the format requires; and there is no boundary integer to exercise for category selection, because there is only one category to land in. Korean's grammatical number-agreement is likewise absent (Address and gender, Header and label forms): the noun following a counted number does not change form regardless of the count.
- **Numbers.** Grouping separator `,` (U+002C), decimal separator `.` (U+002E): `2000` -> "2,000", `12000` -> "12,000", `1250.5` -> "1,250.5", `1250000` -> "1,250,000" — the same Western grouping as `en`, unlike `ro`'s reversed pair. Four-digit numbers **are** grouped.
- **Money.** `AppCurrency.format` gives "KRW 120" and "KRW 1,250" with a no-break space (U+00A0) between the ISO code and the number, because KRW is outside the narrow-symbol allow-list (Engineering flags, above); the locale's own standard form is "₩120" / "₩1,250" — KRW has no minor unit, so neither form ever shows decimals. Compact: the app prints "KRW 1.25k" / "KRW 1.25m" / "KRW 1.25b" where Korean itself would write "KRW 1.25천" for thousands and, critically, "KRW 125만" for 1,250,000 — a different base unit (10,000, not 1,000,000), not just a different word (Engineering flags). Never write a currency name into copy.
- **Percent** is glued after the number with no space: "40%", "100%" (U+0025). "%lld%%" and "-%lld%%" keep that shape.
- **Time is 12-hour** (`Locale.hourCycle` h12): "오전 9:41", "오후 11:59" — the 오전/오후 (AM/PM) marker comes **before** the time, the reverse of English's trailing marker, so any string built by concatenating a marker after a formatted time is a bug in Korean, not a style choice. The twelve `en` clock literals (`12AM`, `06PM` and kin on `insights.appUsage.{axisMidnight,axisNoon,axisSixAm,axisSixPm}`, `profile.appUsage.axis.*`, `screenTimeReport.{axisMidnight,axisNoon,axisSixAM,axisSixPM}`) are chart-axis labels, not clock-face time; **Decided**: render these as bare 24-hour numerals ("00:00", "06:00", "12:00", "18:00") rather than repeating "오전"/"오후" eight times across one chart axis, matching the 24-hour convention `ro` already uses for the same keys. Regular clock-face time elsewhere in the app keeps the 12-hour "오전/오후 h:mm" form.
- **Dates**: "2026년 12월 31일" (year-month-day, each with its own particle, no comma); weekday "화요일"; the combined weekday-and-date form puts the weekday **after** the month and day, the reverse of English's leading weekday: "5월 12일 화요일" — a string that concatenates a weekday prefix onto a date, as some English-shaped code paths do, produces the wrong order in Korean.
- **Ranges.** The two `en`-dash range keys (`onboarding.personalGoal.validation` "1–280 characters", `widgets.startFocus.description` "5–120 minutes") and the one plain-hyphen range (`onboarding.deviceUsage.oneToThreeHours` "1-3 hours") are proposed as `1~280자`, `5~120분`, `1~3시간`: Korean typography conventionally uses the tilde `~` for a numeric range rather than a dash. This is agent knowledge, not a fetched or Apple-runtime citation in this run, and should be confirmed against a native reference before the pilot batch.
- **Quotation marks are decided, not left open, because the evidence agrees.** Foundation reports `“ ”` (U+201C/U+201D) as the primary delimiter for `ko_KR` and `‘ ’` (U+2018/U+2019) as the alternate (locale-formats `quotes`), and Apple's own Korean UI uses the alternate single form specifically to wrap a mentioned app or feature name inside a sentence: "‘%@’ 앱이 스크린 타임에 접근하려고 함" (`FamilyControlsAuthenticationUI.app | Localizable.strings | NOTIFICATION_AUTH_REQUEST_TITLE`), "‘%@’에서 알림을 보내고자 합니다." (`UserNotificationsServer.framework | Localizable.strings | USER_NOTIFICATION_PERMISSION_ALERT_TITLE`). **Rule: `“ ”` for a real displayed quotation (the Quotes feature: `moneyfesting.details.quoteWrapper`, `notifications.contentExtension.quoteDecoration`, `moneyfesting.main.quotePlaceholder`, `theme.createTheme.sampleQuote`, `widgets.quotes.placeholderText`) and `‘ ’` for a UI label or app/feature name mentioned inline (the 11 `en` keys that quote a UI label inside a sentence: `component.errorView.message`, `onboarding.dataMigration.errorMessage`, `theme.picker.errorMessage`, `onboarding.alert.screenTimeMessage`, `focusTask.picker.emptyMessage`, and the mock alert titles)**, matching Apple's own practice exactly rather than needing an owner call.
- **How numerals attach to nouns.** A digit and its counter noun are glued with no space by default: "25분", "1시간 45분" (no space before 분 in either position), "%@에 %@" — this follows the exception in Korean orthography that permits (though does not strictly require) omitting the space between an Arabic numeral and a following unit/counter noun, cited from the national language body's own guidance on the rule: "단위를 나타내는 명사가 아라비아 숫자 뒤에 붙을 때"… "아라비아 숫자와 어울리어 쓰이는 경우에는 붙여 쓸 수 있다" (한글 맞춤법 제43항, https://www.korean.go.kr/front/onlineQna/onlineQnaView.do?mn_id=216&qna_seq=316004, fetched 2026-09-16, citing the standard example "7미터"). Native-numeral counters (개, 번) and Sino-Korean counters (분, 초, 일, %) attach the same way; see Short forms and units for why the native-versus-Sino-Korean numeral-word choice does not actually arise anywhere in this digit-driven UI.
- A caveat for `scripts/term_audit.py` and any future Korean grep stem: Korean verb conjugation elides a stem's final consonant in some forms (예: 벌다 "to earn" -> 법니다, not 벌+ㅂ니다 literally; the ㄹ also drops before the attributive/gerund `-는` and the past-attributive `-ㄴ`, giving 버는 and 번, neither of which contains the literal substring "벌" either), so a literal substring stem can miss a legitimately conjugated banned word. The Money frame table below picks stems from forms that do contain the literal substring, names 법니다, 버는 and 번 as forms the stem misses, and flags this limitation once rather than re-deriving every irregular conjugation.

## Length trigger

- **1.05×, measured by display width, not character count.** `scripts/catalog_check.py` C5 puts a Korean string longer than 1.05 times its English width on the render list; the ratio is measured from Apple's own Korean translations across 45,067 unique English/Korean pairs in the iOS 26.1 runtime (display width, East Asian Wide/Fullwidth glyphs counted as 2 columns), and 1.05× is the value, rounded to 0.05, that matches the same statistic Romanian's 1.35× calibrates against (Apple's `ro` exceeds 1.35× in 31.6% of pairs; Apple's `ko` exceeds 1.05× in 30.8%, `unrounded_share_match` 1.045; length-stats `recommendation`). **Character count is the wrong metric here**: on raw character length, Korean's own median ratio is 0.538 (Korean text is usually shorter in character count than English, because one Hangul syllable block often carries what takes English several letters), and the character-count trigger that would match the same 31.6% statistic is 0.64× — a number that would flag almost everything and is useless as a fit signal. Width is the metric because a single wide Hangul glyph occupies roughly twice the horizontal space of a Latin letter even when the character count is lower.
- **Korean generally compresses, but short and all-caps strings are the exception.** Median display-width ratio overall is 0.923 (below 1: Korean usually renders narrower than English), but the tail inverts for short strings: English strings of 1-10 characters have a median width ratio of 0.9 and a p90 of 1.6, with 17.56% exceeding 1.35× — these are exactly the short eyebrows and one-word headers (`achievements.streak.title` "Streak", `insights.mood.mood` "MOOD"). All-caps English strings of 1-10 characters are the worst case: 41.32% exceed 1.35× in width and 5.11% exceed 2×, because a short all-caps caption forces the render to hold several full-width Hangul glyphs where the English original held a handful of narrow capitals (length-stats `all_caps`).
- No catalog share can be quoted for `ko`: there are 0 Korean units at the snapshot. Re-state this line with a real share after the first wave.
- Counts never establish fit. Verdicts come from render evidence against each view's own floor and line limit (`references/surface-budgets.md`, `references/render-evidence.md`). Shortening cuts words, never facts; accessibility strings need no separate shortening pass in Korean, because they already carry the same compact digit-plus-counter form as the visible text (Short forms and units).
